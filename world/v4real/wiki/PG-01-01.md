# PG-01-01: IT Security Procedural Guide: Identification and Authentication (IA), Revision 8

## 1 Introduction

Identification and Authentication (I&A) is used when referring to processes, features, or mechanisms used to
implement the IA family of controls. The mechanisms associated with I&A, when effectively applied, ensure that users
or devices are uniquely identified and that their claimed identity is verified before access is allowed.

Account mechanisms are when a user or device is provided a unique name in a system. The most commonly known
authentication mechanisms are usernames coupled with passwords. Passwords are persistent shared secrets. Quillmark
requires strong authentication mechanisms for all systems.

Every system must follow the practices described in this guide. Any deviations, exceptions, or other conditions not
following company policies and standards must be approved by the CISO (POL-100 1.6).

## 2 Multi-factor authentication

Authentication schemes for all systems and users must utilize MFA using two or more types of authentication factors.
Systems should integrate with the company's central sign-on service, which manages the MFA options available to
users.

Table 2-1. Allowable MFA Methods

| Authentication Method | Example | Phishing Resistant? |
|---|---|---|
| Security key (FIDO2 / WebAuthn hardware key) | Quillmark-issued key on a lanyard; required for administrator accounts | Yes |
| WebAuthn platform authenticator | Laptop fingerprint reader | Yes |
| Push-based notifications as the 2nd factor | Authenticator app on a registered phone. Push notifications are verifier-compromise resistant; however, phishing resistance is the go-to target state. | No |
| Time-based One Time Passwords using a mobile application | Authenticator app codes | No |
| One-time code sent by the desk under an MFA exception | See Appendix B, exemption (4) | No |

Privileged and administrator accounts must use phishing-resistant methods only (see GUI-PAM).

## 3 Authenticator management

### 3.1 IA-05 Authenticator Management

Control: Manage system authenticators by:
a. Verifying, as part of the initial authenticator distribution, the identity of the individual, group, role,
   service, or device receiving the authenticator;
b. Establishing initial authenticator content for any authenticators issued by the organization;
c. Ensuring that authenticators have sufficient strength of mechanism for their intended use;
d. Establishing and implementing administrative procedures for initial authenticator distribution, for lost or
   compromised or damaged authenticators, and for revoking authenticators;
e. Changing default authenticators prior to first use;
f. Changing or refreshing authenticators [when compromised, recovered/forgotten, or as required by the system];
g. Protecting authenticator content from unauthorized disclosure and modification.

**Implementation guidance: password administration.**

● Disable user accounts as quickly as possible when the user is no longer authorized resource access. Delete an
account once files associated with the account have been reviewed and reassigned or removed.

● Change passwords when a compromise is suspected.

● Reset passwords. When resetting passwords, user identity must be verified. This can be done either in person or
having the user answer questions for which the answers are only expected to be known by the user (i.e., not publicly
available or easily attainable) that can be compared to the correct answers in the administrator's database.
Passwords forgotten by their owner should be changed, not reissued.

**Changing/Refreshing Passwords.** Users must be authenticated before resetting or distributing a password.
Self-service password resets are commonly available in web based applications and identity management software. The
function allows users who have forgotten passwords or inadvertently locked out their account to repair/reset it
themselves without involvement of the help desk. The feature expedites problem resolution involving password resets
but can also introduce new attack vectors that could result in system compromise if implemented insecurely.
Authenticators must be changed for group or role accounts when membership to those accounts changes.

The company does not encourage the use of knowledge-based questions in self-service password reset functions. The
use of such functions must adhere to NIST SP 800-63A regarding the use of knowledge-based verification of identity.

**Desk verification.** The methods the desk may use to verify identity before a credential action are listed in
POL-100 Chapter 2. The `verify_identity` tool also offers `knowledge_questions`, retained for systems still on
revision P processes (see Appendix B).

**Lost, compromised or damaged authenticators.** A lost or stolen authenticator must be revoked and reported as set
out in GUI-LOST. A forgotten authenticator (known to be safe, for example left at home) need not be revoked.

### 3.2 IA-11 Re-authentication

Control: Require users to re-authenticate when [passwords are reset, privileged functions are executed, or after
the inactivity timeframes listed in PG-01-07 AC-11 and AC-12].

### 3.3 IA-12 Identity Proofing

Control:
a. Identity proof users that require accounts for logical access to systems based on appropriate identity assurance
   level requirements;
b. Resolve user identities to a unique individual; and
c. Collect, validate, and verify identity evidence.
